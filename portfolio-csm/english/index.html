Blizzard Case Deep Dive

Deep DiveDelivering a firm refusal without losing the customer

Problem

A refund the terms ruled out

  • The ask: refund an in‑game item.
  • The terms: items apply instantly — non-refundable.
  • The gap: terms only visible via an external link.

Escalation

Three rounds, same answer, rising frustration

  • Round 1: "no refund" — the customer came back angrier.
  • Round 2: "When did you ever show me the terms?"
  • Round 3: "Why design it against the customer?" — the policy answer had run out.

Resolution

The best possible answer

  • Honesty: the terms hide behind a link because they're too long for the game UI.
  • Commitment: proposed an in‑purchase preview of the refund clause to the dev team.
  • Apology: for the refund itself — not for the misunderstanding.

Lessons

What I felt and learned

  • Felt: being right three times can still be a failing conversation.
  • Learned: the real question was "why wasn't I warned" — answer that one.
  • Learned: when the answer must be no, give a reason, a fix, and an apology.

Blizzard Case Deep Dive

Deep DiveHow a repeated ticket became a US HQ-approved standard

Problem

A rule that punished honest mistakes

  • The rule: refunds only within 7 days.
  • The case: paid on the wrong account, couldn't log in, window closed.
  • By the book: "reject" — correct by policy, wrong by common sense.

Judgment

Finding the structural cause

  • Shift: read the pattern, not single tickets.
  • Evidence: zero logins on the charged account — mis‑payment, not abuse.
  • Criterion: allow a late refund when the account shows no access history.

Execution

From proposal to standard

  • Documented: every recurrence — data, not anecdotes.
  • Approved: team review first, then a formal request to US HQ.
  • Adopted: now part of the standard refund procedure.

Lessons

What I felt and learned

  • Felt: the policy line and the customer's side aren't opposites.
  • Learned: documentation turns a complaint into a decision.
  • Learned: organizations move on organized evidence, not loud frustration.

Deep DiveMoving a vendor from a price demand to a shared target

Conflict

Two sides, two languages

  • The vendor: "costs went up — the fee should too."
  • The hotel: "answered calls are reservations — service first."
  • The gap: same table, no shared definition of "fair."

Approach

Making the data speak both languages

  • Common ground: numbers both sides already trusted — inbound, answered, abandoned calls.
  • Reframe: not "fee up or down" — "what service level earns what."
  • Tone: no blame for the past, only a clear bar for the future.

Agreement

An offer the vendor could accept

  • Terms: fee holds — +2.5% after 3 months above a 90% response rate.
  • For them: a real path to the raise — proof instead of pleading.
  • For us: cost control for management, a readable target for operations.

Lessons

What I felt and learned

  • Felt: the conflict ends when the metric becomes the referee.
  • Learned: "earn more by proving more" lands very differently from "no."
  • Learned: a partner stays a partner when the deal leaves them a way to win.

Deep DiveResearching a market like a BDR — before any outreach

Question

Could I map a market with no inside data?

  • The target: AICC — AI contact centers — a market I'd never sold into.
  • The rule: public data only — nothing proprietary.
  • The goal: know the pain before writing a message.

Research

Public data, read like a territory

  • Classified: 29,872 public CS inquiries into 8 issue types.
  • Crossed: each type scored on two axes — volume × risk.
  • Benchmarked: 5 AICC vendors — product pages, consultation forms, disclosed metrics.

Segmentation

Not everything should be automated

  • Automate: high-volume, low-risk — billing, account, authentication.
  • Protect: incidents and complaints need QA and expert routing first.
  • Message: an efficiency angle and a risk-control angle — one pitch never fits both.

Lessons

What I felt and learned

  • Felt: with the data mapped, I could open a conversation I had no history in.
  • Learned: segmentation is the message — "automate everything" persuades no one.
  • Learned: discovery questions write themselves once the data is mapped.

Team Project · AI Vision

Deep DiveReframing the problem from obstacles to the journey

Problem

A demo about features, not people

  • The build: AI that detects obstacles on tactile paving.
  • The drift: the concept over-indexed on detection — and missed the walk itself.
  • The user: a visually impaired walker needs to arrive, not just be warned.

Reframe

From detection to journey

  • Asked: what does the user need to finish a walk?
  • Found: destination movement, route recovery, less interaction burden.
  • Chose: "AI helps continue the journey" over "AI detects obstacles."

Design

Decisions that followed the reframe

  • Voice-first: risk alert, guidance, route recovery — hands and eyes stay free.
  • Operator view: risk image, location, and detection records for quality review.
  • Metrics: guidance delay, route deviation, duplicate TTS.

Lessons

What I felt and learned

  • Felt: the first idea is usually the feature, not the problem.
  • Learned: reframing the question changed every decision after it.
  • Learned: a PoC sells when it shows a journey, not a model.

SAFE

Team Project · DeToks

Deep DivePositioning an LLM tool around cost, not compression

Problem

Paying twice for the same work

  • The waste: LLM CLI workflows re-inject the same context — tokens, latency, cost.
  • The limit: compression doesn't cut the number of calls.
  • The catch: reuse only creates value when it's safe to reuse.

A/B Test

Two stories for the same tool

  • A — Compression: "we compress repeated prompts to cut token usage."
  • B — Safe reuse: "we cut repeated calls by judging when reuse is safe."
  • Criteria: pain clarity, value, follow-up potential.

Decision

Why B won

  • Connection: B spoke to budget governance, speed, and quality-risk control.
  • Difference: A described a mechanism; B, a decision buyers recognize.
  • Design followed: task graph → similarity scoring → cache/RAG gate.

Lessons

What I felt and learned

  • Felt: engineers loved A; budget owners heard B.
  • Learned: the same architecture supports very different stories — pick the buyer's.
  • Learned: AI cost talk lands as governance, not optimization.
